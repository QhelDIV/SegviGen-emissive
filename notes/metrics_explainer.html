What are we actually doing?

A plain-language map of the current Lightgen experiment — the task, the scoreboard, and every term flying around the war room (flat mean, Otsu, strata, W5/W1…). No prior familiarity with this project's jargon assumed.

Short answer: yes — we are fine-tuning SegviGen. Specifically its full_seg model. We're giving it a narrower job than usual: instead of splitting a shape into many labeled parts, it just has to paint every surface voxel white (this glows / is emissive) or black (it doesn't). Everything below explains how we check whether the fine-tune is working, and what we've found so far.

★Numbers to beat

We score every prediction with IoU (intersection-over-union): overlap between the voxels the model painted white and the voxels that are actually emissive in the ground truth, divided by the union of the two. 1 = perfect overlap, 0 = no overlap at all. This is a SegviGen-vs-SegviGen project — the reference scores below are both SegviGen itself, not an outside baseline:

Oracle: no training at all — take the PRETRAINED SegviGen's existing part segmentation, label each part "emissive" if >50% of it overlaps ground truth. This is the bar fine-tuning has to beat to be worth doing. Best epoch of our previous fine-tune (before this dataset/loss work), before it broke

Success = a fine-tuned SegviGen beating both of these numbers, with qualitatively good white/black maps on shapes that actually glow — not just a higher average. Our fine-tune already matched the oracle once, then destroyed itself a few epochs later. The rest of this page is about fixing that failure mode and finding out whether the fix actually helps.

1The task, pictured

white = predicted emissive (glowing) black = predicted not emissive solid = required input · dashed = optional (photo)

The ground truth comes from the original artists: whichever parts of a 3D asset they assigned an emissive material (a glowing texture, like a screen or a light strip) are the "true white" voxels. The model never sees that material assignment directly — it only sees the shape's geometry and its own PBR texture, plus an optional rendered photo that we found helps, and has to guess which surface regions glow. Where does that optional photo input actually come from, and why keep it if it's "optional"? See the next section.

2Where the "optional" photo actually comes from: paper vs. code

We checked the published method (arXiv 2603.16869 §3.3.2) against the shipped SegviGen code. They disagree about this exact input:

The paper's full-seg mode feeds the flow transformer only the shape latent and all-zero point tokens — by the text, "purely native 3D representation," no image anywhere. The shipped code takes the input GLB, self-renders a photo of it, strips the background, and runs it through DINOv3 into the same transformer's cross-attention — a real extra input channel the paper never mentions. That gap exists because the underlying TRELLIS.2 texture flow is image-conditioned by architecture; the authors kept that slot fed rather than retraining it away. Takeaway: the photo adds no outside information (it's rendered from the asset itself), but the pretrained weights rely on the channel being filled — which is why our fine-tune keeps it on.

3Scoring a prediction: IoU

IoU punishes both kinds of mistake symmetrically: painting white where it should be black (false positive) and painting black where it should be white (false negative) both shrink the overlap relative to the union. That symmetry is exactly what makes it a trap for tiny emissive regions — see below.

4Why models "give up" and paint everything black

~11%average emissive voxels across shapes
1.4%median training shape's emissive fraction
24.5%of shapes have ZERO emissive voxels

Because emissive voxels are rare, a model that paints every voxel black gets ~89% accuracy — it "looks" like it's doing great — but scores 0 on our actual metric, since there's zero overlap with the true white region. That "paint all black" answer is a seductive shortcut for the optimizer to fall into. This is exactly the failure that hit our previous fine-tune: it hit 0.230 IoU at epoch 2, then collapsed — both training and validation scores slid toward zero together as the whole model drifted to all-black predictions, bottoming out at 0.035 IoU by epoch 6. That rules out classical overfitting (where training would keep improving while validation falls) — this is the whole model giving up on the minority class.

5The fix under test: W5 vs W1

To stop the model from taking the all-black shortcut, we changed the training loss so a mistake on an emissive voxel counts for more: weight w = 1 + (W−1)·(emissive fraction of that cell). W5 is the run with that fix turned on (mistakes on emissive voxels count 5× as much). W1 is an identical control run with the weight left at 1 — i.e. the old plain loss, same data, same schedule. Running both side by side is the only way to know whether any improvement is really caused by the weighting. Exact loss math: the fine-tune predictions page.

The good news: neither run collapsed this time — 18 epochs, no crash to zero, for either weighting. That instability is fixed. The catch: 0.215 vs 0.221 is statistically indistinguishable — both close to each other and to the old 0.230 peak. On this 16-shape quick-validation slice, the weighting hasn't proven itself yet.

6The real discovery: glow size, not material

A separate test — training on just 10 shapes until the model should be able to memorize each one — found the split isn't about PBR material type as first suspected. It's about how much of the shape's surface actually glows:

emissive (white) voxels non-emissive (black) voxels

Big glowing regions get memorized perfectly, even at just 600 training steps. Shapes with a tiny glow — like one small LED on an otherwise plain machine — stay near zero no matter how long we train on them: missing that one tiny bright spot scores exactly 0 IoU, same as missing the whole thing. We also directly checked whether the VAE (the model's latent encoder/decoder) was silently destroying tiny glow regions before training even sees them — it isn't: even a region covering just 0.03% of the surface survives encoding and decoding faithfully. The ceiling is coming from the objective/training dynamics, not from lossy compression.

7How we're reading the final results

A larger evaluation — all 111 validation shapes, across 4 checkpoints — is now complete. It reports numbers three different ways, specifically so a single misleading average can't hide what's really happening:

Flat mean — the plain average IoU over all 111 shapes, each scored at its best fixed cutoff. This is the single headline number, and (as the next section explains) it's the one most easily gamed by a model that plays it safe.

The same scores, split into these 4 groups, are the per-bucket strata — "strata" just means slices of the data grouped by a shared trait (here, glow size). This shows exactly where the model wins and loses, rather than blending a strong "large glow" score and a weak "tiny glow" score into one number that looks mediocre everywhere.

The model's raw output per voxel is a grayish confidence value, not a clean 0/1 — so a threshold decides the white/black cutoff:

We sweep several fixed cutoffs (0.2–0.5) and use Otsu's method — an automatic threshold that finds the best split point separately for each shape — so a badly-chosen fixed cutoff can't be mistaken for a bad model.

Four checkpoints get all of the above: W5-best, W1-best, and each one's EMA sibling — an exponential moving average of the model's weights over recent training steps, kept alongside the raw checkpoint because it's often slightly smoother/steadier.

All four checkpoints score below the 0.230 / 0.235 reference lines on flat mean — but reading the buckets, not just the flat mean, is the whole point of the next section.

The zero-glow scoring trap

25 of the 111 validation shapes have zero true emissive voxels — nothing on them glows. On those shapes, a model that predicts nothing scores a perfect IoU of 1.0; a model that paints anything at all there scores 0. That single rule quietly rewards timidity: a model that plays it safe and predicts little/nothing racks up free points on a quarter of the validation set, inflating its flat mean, while a model that actually attempts to segment glowing surfaces can look worse on paper even when it's the better segmenter.

Flat means: W1-EMA 0.172 vs W5-EMA 0.096 — the "timid" checkpoint wins the headline number despite being worse where it actually matters (large-glow shapes).

One consequence: whatever process picks a "best" checkpoint needs to look at the buckets, not just the flat mean, or it will systematically prefer timid models. A second honest caveat: per-shape predictions are unstable across checkpoints — the same shape can score 0.0 under one checkpoint and 0.5 under another, because generation is stochastic. Averaging several sampling runs per shape at eval time is a known next step, not yet done for the numbers above.

What's next

The final buckets confirm the pattern the glow-size discovery predicted: the tiny-glow bucket is weak everywhere (0.01–0.06, across all four checkpoints) regardless of weighting, while the large-glow bucket is where a real gap shows up — W5-EMA reaches 0.52 there, well above every other checkpoint. That points at two next steps rather than "train longer": (1) a stronger per-shape balanced weighting, where each shape's positive (emissive) voxels get a fixed share of the loss regardless of how tiny the glow region is, and (2) fixing checkpoint/model selection itself so it can't be won by a timid model farming free points on zero-glow shapes — e.g. selecting on the non-zero-glow subset, not the flat mean. The plan is to keep reporting the stratified numbers to the team alongside the flat mean, rather than let one average hide where the model actually stands.
