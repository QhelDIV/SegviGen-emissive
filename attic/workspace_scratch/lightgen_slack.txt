My update on the visualization of the filtering metric.
First, let's look at the effect of f001-score filtering.
The bottom plot shows the CDF: percentage of the object covered (y axis) by <= f001-score (x  axis)
We divide 0~1 into 5 intervals, for each intervals we sample 3 shapes and visualize.
As we can clearly see, as the f001 score drops, the reconstructed shape (middle of the subfigure) is far from the gt shape (right).
Left subimage shows the patch layout.image.png [8:45 PM](Please zoom in to see more details)
[8:46 PM]Here we show more examples (10 samples per interval)
image.png [8:49 PM]Next we show the visualization of the filtering rubric of discrepancy:
(#n_patch_original - #n_patch_eval).abs()
if this is 0, meaning no patch errorly added/removed after the entire processing.
This visualization below shows that the larger the discrepancy, larger number of patches the shape typically have, and geometry quality is typically influenced by the processing error.[8:49 PM]image.png Xingguang (Kevin) Yan  [9:02 PM]
In conclusion, f001_score is clearly indicating the geometry quality.
But discrepancy of n_patch does not clearly indicate the UV quality or geometry quality.
To me, it is an overkill (maybe 1 or 2 patches are missing after processing, but the overall geometry is good).
It serves as a hard rule to make sure strange things not happening.

Below are number of patches histograms showing distribution of #n_patch_original, #n_patch_eval, #n_patch_eval(filtered)
One thing to notice is that our processing code forces to keep only the largest 512 patches.
However, #n_patch_eval shows there are many cases that the processed shape still have >512 patches, many of them even > 1000.
This indicates clearly something is wrong.

After forcing #n_patch_original == #n_patch_eval, we have a much sensible distribution, where almost all shapes have <=512 patches. (edited)

Teaser really tells people what the proposed method enables.

We know lightgen will turn on 'lights' to provide illumination.

For example, I found that to make the emission provide enough illumination to the environment, it should be very bright (say, emission strength = 100). In this case, the textures/color of the emission on the objects just seems pure white. This is something we can't know by drawing by our hands.

Given this observation, we may want to ask:

To make a shape part a proper light, shall we care more about segmentation, rather than textures?

We also want to ask:
For the emissive map generation itself:
Does it really make sense to turn a fan's blade emissive, if our goal is to make part of the surface region emissive for an arbitrary 3d shape.

If simply deal with LDR light emission map enough?

Light strength is not just a finite number between [0,1], it also in many cases not just a 'diffuse emission' that we assume.

Lighting is also about the way we are 'seeing' it. What makes the 'RawNeRF' results so cool is not only about the emission, but also how we are perceiving it - the camera exposure, the dynamic range, ... Without taking the perceiver into account, we won't produce images that feel right.(edited)

For TEXGen and SeqTEX, they both are trying to overcome the limitation of UV parameterization: Surface regions that are adjacent in 3D can be far apart in UV space. But they are using different strategies:

TEXGen:  the UV features are lifted to the 3D surfaces, processed in 3D space, and then projected back to UV for diffusion denoising.
SeqTEX: the multi-view image tokens and UV tokens are jointly denoised via a video diffusion model with the guidance of some geometry cues.

I am not quite sure if they should belong to the same family or not: they are trying to solve the same limitation of UV parameterization but with different strategy.

By observing and thinking about the texture generation works.
I found that not so many people are paying attention to the 'interaction' part of the problem.
Current paradigm is mostly 'image to texture' or 'prompt to texture'.

One just can not create amazing textures by just edit the text/image prompt.

Along this line, I believe we can think more and possibly observe how artists create really good textures in practice.

Material aware interactive local semantic segmentation for texture generation and editing. (edited)

image.png Dongchen  [1:29 AM]
One observation from the 10-sample overfit run on finetuning the TRELLIS.2's PBR VAE: the model struggles to produce exact-zero values in non-emissive regions.

In our previous visualization the --threshold 0.05 filter hides this. But if I drop the threshold, dark floating particles appear everywhere.

A simple threshold works at small scale, but at larger scale I expect the "dark particle" density to rise and more varied scenes means more edge cases where the lowest value for such dark particles exceeds a simple fixed threshold, and a threshold high enough to catch them also starts removing the actual emission.

If those non zero dark particles are there and we don't care about them, as we increase the emission strength, the entire object will have dim light, which is unacceptable I think.

Similar problem also exists in TEXGen pipeline, where those UV maps look clean for those non-emissive regions, but they are actually not zero. For example, the second image looks very clean (it is from the 10 sample overfitting test from TEXGen). But if we times by 20 (2n image), there will be some noise show up, then even more with 50 (3rd image).

I think this is could be common failure mode for those methods. What do you think? (edited)

The main flow is:
texture generation --> texture generation does not model underlying physical properties (PBR properties) --> material generation -- material generation does not model the emission property --> concurrent work EmissionGen ---> but their multiview-based method is fundamentally not suitable for emission generation --> why ours

Object-centric Inverse rendering --> did not model emission --> scene level inverse rendering models emission -->for scene level, we  can rely on observations about how emitter interact with the environment --> but object level, there is little observation of such interaction --> we need to use generation based method (ours) to generate plausible light (edited)
